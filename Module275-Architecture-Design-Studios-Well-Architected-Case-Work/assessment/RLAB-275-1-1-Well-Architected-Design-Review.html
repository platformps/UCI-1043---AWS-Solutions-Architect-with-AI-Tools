Introduction

A solutions architect does not start with services. The architect starts with what the client needs, turns those needs into decisions, checks the decisions against the six pillars of the AWS Well-Architected Framework, and explains the result to people who will challenge it.

In this lab, your team works on a small, simple client case. You write the requirements, choose an AWS design, draw it, look for risks, explain the trade-offs that you accept, and defend your design in a short design review. The case is small on purpose: the goal is to practice the way of thinking, not to use as many services as possible.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lab, learners will be able to:

  • Classify client business requirements as functional or non-functional, and give each one a measure.
  • Translate business requirements into AWS architecture decisions.
  • Apply the six pillars of the AWS Well-Architected Framework to identify risks and improvement opportunities.
  • Produce an architecture diagram that shows the data flow and the failure domains.
  • Justify trade-offs between security, reliability, performance, and cost.
  • Defend a solution in a structured design review.

  • A diagramming tool, such as diagrams.net, Microsoft PowerPoint, or Google Slides. You can also draw by hand and take a photo.
  • The AWS Architecture Icons, if your tool does not include them: AWS Architecture Icons.

Scenario and Instructions

Your team works for a small cloud consulting company. A new client, BrightBite Bakery, asks for help. Read the client brief, then complete the parts in order. Each part produces one piece of your final design document.

Teams: Work in a team of five learners. The team size can vary with the size of the class, and your instructor decides the teams.

Client brief: BrightBite Bakery (an invented company for this lab)

  • BrightBite Bakery has three shops in one city. Customers call the shops to order cakes, and the phone lines are busy every Friday and Saturday morning.
  • The owner wants a website where customers can see the menu with photos, order a cake for a pickup date, and pay online.
  • Payments go through an external payment provider. The bakery must not store card numbers.
  • The website stores each customer’s name, email address, and phone number, and the order history. The order history must be kept for two years.
  • Most orders arrive on Friday and Saturday mornings. On weekday nights, the site is almost unused.
  • The owner says: “If the website is down on a Saturday morning, we lose a day of orders. It must be back within 4 hours, and we cannot lose more than 1 hour of orders.” In architecture terms, 4 hours is the recovery time objective (RTO), the longest time the site can be down, and 1 hour is the recovery point objective (RPO), the most data, measured in time, that can be lost.
  • The bakery has one part-time developer and no operations staff.
  • The budget is small, and the owner wants to know what the website will cost each month.
  • Shop staff need to see the day’s orders on a tablet in each shop.

  1. For each requirement, decide how AWS will meet it. Choose from the following services, or explain why you need another one:
    NeedServices to consider

    Application logicAWS Lambda with Amazon API Gateway, or Amazon EC2 with an Application Load Balancer and Auto Scaling

    Sign-in for shop staffAmazon Cognito

DecisionRequirements it meets (#)Why this choicePillars it supports

Tip: Use the keywords in the brief. “Almost unused at night” and “small budget” point toward pay-per-use services. “No operations staff” points toward managed services. “Back within 4 hours” and “lose at most 1 hour” decide your backup and recovery design.

Evidence 2: Your completed decision table.

Part 3: Draw the Architecture Diagram

  1. Draw your design with AWS Architecture Icons. Your diagram must show:
    1. The AWS Region and at least two Availability Zones, where your design uses them.
    2. Every AWS service from Part 2, with a label.
    3. The data flow of one customer order, as numbered arrows (1, 2, 3, and so on), from the customer’s browser to the stored order and to the shop tablet.
    4. The external payment provider, outside the AWS boundary.
    5. A failure domain note: mark one component or one Availability Zone that could fail, and write in one sentence what happens to the orders when it does.
  2. Under the diagram, write the numbered steps of the data flow in one line each, for example: “1. The customer opens the menu page from CloudFront.”
  3. Check the diagram: a person who has not read the brief should understand how an order travels and where it is stored.

Evidence 3: Your architecture diagram, with the numbered data flow and the failure domain note.

Part 4: Apply Well-Architected Thinking

  1. For each pillar, answer the question in the following table about your design, in one or two sentences.
  2. Give each answer a risk level: High, Medium, or None.
  3. For every High or Medium risk, write one improvement.
PillarQuestionYour answerRiskImprovement
Operational excellenceHow will the part-time developer know that the site has a problem, and how will they change it safely?
SecurityHow is customer data protected, who can see the orders, and how do you avoid storing card numbers?
ReliabilityWhat happens if one Availability Zone fails on a Saturday morning? How do you meet the 4-hour RTO and the 1-hour RPO?
Performance efficiencyHow does the site handle the Friday and Saturday morning peak, and how do the photos load quickly?
Cost optimizationWhat do you pay for on a quiet weekday night, and how will the owner see the monthly cost?
SustainabilityWhich resources sit idle when no one is ordering, and how could you reduce them?

Optional: In the Well-Architected Tool

If you have an AWS account, you can also record this review in the AWS Well-Architected Tool, which is free to use. No resources are deployed.

  1. Open the AWS Well-Architected Tool console, and choose Define workload.
  2. Name it, for example “BrightBite Online Orders”, and enter a description, the environment, and the Region. The AWS Well-Architected Framework lens is selected automatically. Save the workload.
  3. Choose Start reviewing. For each pillar question, select the best practices that your design follows, mark the questions that do not apply, and add notes that explain your choices.
  4. Open the improvement plan to see the high and medium risks that the tool found.
  5. Choose Save milestone, and name it, for example “v1 – Initial design review”.
  6. Choose Generate report to download the PDF.

Evidence 4: Your completed pillar review table, with at least two risks and their improvements.

Part 5: Justify the Trade-Offs

  1. Choose the two most important decisions in your design, for example the database choice and how you meet the RTO and RPO.
  2. For each decision, write a short architecture decision record (ADR) with these four parts:
    1. Context: the requirement or the problem.
    2. Options: at least two options that you considered.
    3. Decision: the option that you chose.
    4. Trade-off: what you gain and what you give up, in terms of security, reliability, performance, and cost. Remember that security is not traded away to save money.
ADR 1: [title]
Context:   [the requirement or problem]
Options:   [option A] / [option B]
Decision:  [the option chosen]
Trade-off: We gain [...]. We accept [...], because [...].

Example:

ADR 1: Database for customers and orders
Context:   Simple orders, two years of history, weekend peaks, quiet nights, small budget,
           and no operations staff.
Options:   (A) Amazon RDS for PostgreSQL, Multi-AZ  /  (B) Amazon DynamoDB, on-demand
Decision:  (B) DynamoDB, on-demand.
Trade-off: We gain pay-per-request cost on quiet nights, automatic scaling at the peak,
           and no database servers to manage. We accept that ad hoc SQL reports are
           harder, because the owner’s reports are simple and can be built from known
           queries or an export to S3.

Evidence 5: Your two ADRs.

Part 6: Present and Defend the Design

Each team presents to another team, which acts as the review board. Then the teams switch roles.

  1. Present your design in 7 minutes, in this order:
    1. The client and the three most important requirements (1 minute).
    2. The diagram, following the numbered data flow of one order (2 minutes).
    3. The two key decisions and their trade-offs, from your ADRs (2 minutes).
    4. The top risks and your improvements (1 minute).
    5. What happens when the failure that you marked in Part 3 occurs (1 minute).
  2. The review board asks questions for 5 minutes. Each reviewer asks at least one question, based on one pillar, for example:
    • “Why did you choose this database instead of the other option?”
    • “What does this design cost on a quiet Tuesday night?”
    • “Where is the customer’s email address stored, and who can read it?”
    • “What exactly happens to an order placed one minute before the failure?”
  3. Answer with reasons from the pillars and the requirements. If you cannot answer a question, say so, and write it down as a follow-up action.
  4. Record every question, your answer, and any follow-up action in the following table.
Question from the review boardPillarOur answerFollow-up action, if any

Evidence 6: Your presentation slides or notes, and your completed design review table.

Part 7: Reflection

Each learner answers these questions individually, in two to four sentences each.

  1. Which requirement in the brief had the biggest effect on your design, and why?
  2. Which question from the review board was the hardest to answer? What would you change in your design because of it?
  3. Describe one trade-off that your team accepted. Would you make the same choice if the bakery had 50 shops instead of three?

Evidence 7: Your individual answers to the three reflection questions.

Submission

  • Individual reflection: each learner writes Evidence 7, the reflection answers, as a separate PDF named FirstName_LastName_RLAB-275.1.1_Reflection.pdf.
  • Every team member must submit the assignment. Each learner uploads both files to Canvas, in the assignment for this lab: the team design document and their own reflection.

Rubric
