1. The Well-Architected Framework

Problem Statement

FlashCart has grown from a small online store to a business with customers on four continents. Each team built its part of the platform in its own way, and nobody can say whether the whole system is secure, reliable, fast, and affordable. The CTO asks the architecture team for a common way to evaluate every design, and for practice in applying it to real cases.

1.1 Why a Framework for Architecture

  • The AWS Certified Solutions Architect – Associate (SAA-C03) exam validates your ability to use AWS technologies to design solutions based on the AWS Well-Architected Framework.
  • Every exam question describes a case: a business need, some constraints, and a few options. To choose well, you need a consistent way to judge a design, not a list of memorized services.
  • The AWS Well-Architected Framework is that way. AWS built it from experience with many customer architectures. It gives a consistent set of best practices to evaluate architectures, and a set of questions to check how well an architecture follows them.
  • The framework answers one question: is this workload well-architected? You learn the best practices, measure the workload against them, and improve where it falls short. Repeating this cycle over time turns a one-time check into continuous improvement.

1.4 General Design Principles

General design principles are applied across all workloads and all pillars. There are also design principles that are specific to each pillar.

  • Stop guessing your capacity needs: scale with demand instead of buying for the peak.
  • Test systems at production scale: create a production-like environment on demand, test it, and remove it.
  • Automate with architectural experimentation in mind: build and change the environment with code, and track every change.
  • Consider evolutionary architectures: design for changing requirements, not for a fixed design.
  • Drive architectures using data: base decisions on metrics, not on guesses.
  • Improve through game days: simulate events, such as a flash sale or a failure, to test the architecture and the team.

The general design principles make sense when you compare the cloud with a traditional data center:

PrincipleTraditional environmentIn the cloud
Stop guessing your capacity needsCapacity is guessed from high-level requirements, often before any code exists.Capacity scales with real demand.
Test systems at production scaleA full copy of production is too expensive, so problems at scale appear only after launch.A production-scale copy is created for a test and removed after it.

Each pillar also has its own pillar-specific design principles, which apply only to that pillar. For example, the security pillar's "prepare for security events" means having incident management policies and processes, running incident response simulations, and using automation to detect, investigate, and recover faster. 1.5 How the Framework Is Built

AWS describes the framework as three components that work together:

  • Content: the guidance to learn from, meaning the pillars, design principles, questions, best practices, and lenses.
  • Tools: the AWS Well-Architected Tool, which measures a workload against that guidance (Tab 3).
  • Data: what a review records about your workloads, which you use to keep improving the workloads and how the team runs them.

The content is more than six pillar names. Each pillar breaks down into smaller parts, and a review works at the level of the smallest part, the best practice.

LevelWhat it isExample
PillarOne of the six areas of the framework.Reliability.
Best practice areaA group of related questions inside a pillar.Failure management.
QuestionAn open question about how the workload works, with an ID made of the pillar code and a number.REL 13. How do you plan for disaster recovery (DR)?
Best practiceOne specific practice that answers part of a question. Each has its own ID and a level of risk if it is missing.REL13-BP01 Define recovery objectives for downtime and data loss.
  • Question REL 13 has five best practices: REL13-BP01 define recovery objectives for downtime and data loss, REL13-BP02 use defined recovery strategies to meet the recovery objectives, REL13-BP03 test disaster recovery implementation to validate the implementation, REL13-BP04 manage configuration drift at the DR site or Region, and REL13-BP05 automate recovery.
  • In a review, you answer each question by marking the best practices that your workload follows. Each best practice that you do not follow becomes a high risk issue (HRI) or a medium risk issue (MRI), depending on the level of risk that AWS gives that best practice.
  • This is why a review finds risks in a consistent way: the risks come from the questions, not from opinion.
  • The answers are not black and white. A best practice that does not fit the context of a workload may not apply, so the team still uses its own architectural judgment, and records the reason in the notes.

1.6 Lenses

  • A lens is an extra set of questions, best practices, and improvement plan for a specific industry or type of technology. It adds to the framework; it does not replace it.
  • The AWS Well-Architected Framework lens applies to every workload, and the Well-Architected Tool applies it by default.
  • The Lens Catalog holds official lenses that AWS creates and maintains, such as the Serverless Applications, Container Build, SaaS, Data Analytics, Generative AI, Financial Services Industry, and Healthcare Industry lenses.
  • A custom lens holds your own pillars, questions, best practices, and improvement plan, for example your company's internal standards. You can share it with other AWS accounts.
  • One workload can have several lenses, up to 20. Choose the lenses that match the workload: FlashCart's container checkout fits the Container Build lens, and CityCare's patient portal fits the Healthcare Industry lens.

1.7 Trade-Offs Between Pillars

  • When you design a workload, you make trade-offs between pillars based on your business context.
  • For example, you might accept lower reliability to reduce cost and environmental impact in a development environment. For a mission-critical system, you might accept a higher cost to get more reliability.
  • Security and operational excellence are generally not traded off against the other pillars.

Source Conflict

One course slide says that the pillars "are not something to balance, or trade-offs, they’re a synergy". The AWS Well-Architected Framework documentation says that you make trade-offs between pillars based on business context, and that security and operational excellence are generally not traded off. This lesson follows the AWS documentation.

2. The Six Pillars in Practice

Problem Statement

The FlashCart architects agree to use the six pillars, but each pillar is a broad idea. They need the design principles behind each one, and the AWS services and practices that put those principles into action, so that they can use them in reviews and in exam questions.

2.1 Operational Excellence

  • Organize teams around business outcomes.
  • Implement observability for actionable insights, with key performance indicators (KPIs) and telemetry.
  • Safely automate where possible, applying the same engineering discipline as for application code, for example with infrastructure as code.
  • Make frequent, small, reversible changes.
  • Refine operations procedures frequently.
  • Anticipate failure, and drive failure scenarios to understand the risk profile.
  • Learn from all operational events and metrics.
  • Use managed services to reduce the operational burden.

Best practice areas: organization, prepare, operate, and evolve.

AWS services: AWS CloudFormation, AWS Systems Manager, Amazon CloudWatch, AWS X-Ray, AWS CloudTrail, AWS Config, and CI/CD services.

Operational excellence is about running the workload well. A workload that is secure, reliable, fast, and cheap still becomes a burden on the business if the team cannot operate it effectively.

Deep dive: the four best practice areas of operational excellence

2.1.1 Organization

  • Set priorities together: involve business, development, and operations teams to weigh external and internal customer needs, governance requirements (the organization's own policies), compliance requirements (such as PCI DSS, FedRAMP, or HIPAA), and threats, kept in a risk registry.
  • Weigh trade-offs, benefits, and risks: for example, speed to market over cost, or launching with a known, mitigated issue to deliver an important feature. Review the priorities as needs change.
  • Know who owns what: an operating model shows which teams build and which teams run the applications and the platform.
  • Build a supporting culture: executive sponsorship; team members empowered to act when outcomes are at risk; escalation early and often; timely, clear, and actionable communication, such as a change calendar or a change freeze before a big sale; experimentation without blame; time for learning, certifications, and cross-training; enough people and tools to avoid overload; and diverse opinions to challenge assumptions.

2.1.2 Prepare

  • Design telemetry: the workload emits metrics (numbers that describe its state), logs (messages about events), and traces (the path of one request across components). Cover the application, the workload (such as API call volume and HTTP status codes), user activity (such as abandoned transactions), dependencies (such as DNS or an external database), and transaction traces.
  • Design for operations: keep everything in version control, test every change (code, infrastructure, configuration, security controls, and procedures), use configuration management and CI/CD, automate patching, prefer immutable infrastructure, share design standards, and use several environments with stricter controls closer to production.
  • Mitigate deployment risks: plan how to go back to a known good state, test with limited deployments such as canary or one-box deployments, deploy to a parallel environment and keep the old one until the new one is confirmed, and automate testing and rollback.
  • Check operational readiness: use a checklist, such as an operational readiness review (ORR), before going live or making a change; make sure enough trained people can support the workload and rotate on call; run pre-mortems to imagine failures in advance; and keep support plans for every vendor and service the workload depends on.

2.1.3 Operate

  • Understand workload health: define KPIs from business and customer outcomes, such as orders placed or abandoned carts. Collect the metrics in one place, set baselines, learn the normal patterns, and alert when outcomes are at risk or when anomalies appear.
  • Understand operations health: measure the operations work itself, for example successful and failed deployments, mean time to detect (MTTD), and mean time to recovery (MTTR). Every alert must be timely, actionable, and linked to a runbook or playbook; alerts with no action lead to alert fatigue.
  • Respond to events: give every alert an owner and a defined response, put events in order by business impact, define escalation paths, get decisions approved in advance where a person must decide, keep a tested communication plan and status dashboards for each audience, and automate responses where possible.

2.1.4 Evolve

  • Learn from every failure: run a post-incident analysis of customer-impacting events, find the contributing factors, and put in place actions that prevent them from happening again.
  • Improve continuously: review each workload against best practices at least once a year, build feedback loops into procedures and workloads, and plan improvements into the normal development cycle.
  • Share knowledge: keep information accurate and searchable, for example in a wiki; review results with other teams and business owners; and share lessons learned across the organization.

2.2 Security

  • Implement a strong identity foundation, with least privilege and separation of duties.
  • Maintain traceability: monitor, alert, and audit actions and changes.
  • Apply security at all layers: edge, VPC, subnet, load balancer, instance, application, and code.
  • Automate security best practices.
  • Protect data in transit and at rest.
  • Keep people away from data.
  • Prepare for security events.

Best practice areas: security foundations, identity and access management, detection, infrastructure protection, data protection, incident response, and application security.

AWS services: IAM, IAM Identity Center, AWS Organizations, AWS KMS, AWS WAF, AWS Shield, Amazon GuardDuty, Amazon Inspector, Amazon Macie, AWS Security Hub, and AWS Secrets Manager.

Security is the ability to protect data, systems, and assets in the cloud. Its best practices apply to every part of a workload, not only to one layer.

Deep dive: the seven best practice areas of security

2.2.1 Security Foundations

  • Shared responsibility model: AWS is responsible for security of the cloud: the hardware, software, networking, and facilities that run AWS services. The customer is responsible for security in the cloud, and how much depends on the service.
  • Separate workloads with accounts: an AWS account is a hard boundary for security, billing, and access. Keep production apart from development and test, and manage the accounts and their guardrails centrally.
  • Protect the root user: it has full access to everything in the account and some policies cannot limit it. Turn off its programmatic access, put strong controls on it, and do not use it for daily work.
  • Operate securely: derive control objectives from compliance needs and a threat model (a register of likely threats and their mitigations), keep up with new threats, and test security controls automatically in the pipelines.

2.2.2 Identity and Access Management

  • Two kinds of identity: human identities (administrators, developers, operators, and users of the application) and machine identities (applications, tools, EC2 instances, and Lambda functions that call AWS services).
  • Manage identities: require strong sign-in with MFA; use temporary credentials instead of long-term ones; keep secrets such as API keys and passwords in a purpose-built service that rotates them; use one central identity provider for the workforce; audit and rotate any long-term credentials; and grant access through groups and attributes, not to individual users.
  • Manage permissions: grant only the actions needed, on specific resources, under specific conditions (for example, developers can create Lambda functions only in one Region). Set guardrails for the whole organization, remove unused permissions, plan an emergency access process, monitor public and cross-account access, share resources safely, and give third parties only temporary, just-in-time access.

2.2.3 Detection

  • Detection finds two things: unexpected configuration changes and unexpected behavior.
  • Keep the logs of services and applications, analyze logs, findings, and metrics in one central place, automate the response to events, and send alerts that a team can act on, each with a runbook or playbook.

2.2.4 Infrastructure Protection

  • Protect networks with Zero Trust: no component trusts another just because it is on the same network. Group components with the same sensitivity into network layers, control traffic at every layer, inspect and filter it, and automate protection from threat intelligence.
  • Protect compute: scan and patch for vulnerabilities, reduce the attack surface by hardening and removing what is not needed, prefer managed services such as Amazon RDS, Lambda, and Amazon ECS, automate protection, remove interactive access so people act at a distance, and validate software with code signing.

2.2.5 Data Protection

  • Classify data by sensitivity before you design: know what data the workload holds, where it is, who owns it, and which legal rules apply. Set controls and a lifecycle (retention, destruction, access, and sharing) for each level.
  • Data at rest: manage keys securely, enforce encryption, automate the checks, enforce access control with least privilege and no public access, and keep people away from the data.
  • Data in transit: manage keys and certificates securely, enforce encryption (only encrypted protocols for sensitive data leaving the VPC), detect unintended data access automatically, for example with Amazon GuardDuty, and authenticate communications with protocols such as TLS or IPsec.

2.2.6 Incident Response

  • Even with strong prevention and detection, plan how to respond. Agree the goals with stakeholders and legal counsel: contain the issue, recover, preserve evidence for forensics, and find the cause.
  • In the cloud, respond where the event happens, copy logs and snapshots to a central security account, fix misconfigurations by redeploying, automate the responses to repeated incidents, and keep people for new or sensitive ones.
  • Prepare, simulate, iterate: name the key people and outside help, write incident plans, prepare forensic tools, give responders access before an incident, turn playbooks into code that events can start, and run game days to practice. Train every user to report suspicious behavior.

2.2.7 Application Security

  • Train builders in secure development, automate security testing through the whole release cycle, run regular penetration tests, and review code manually so the author is not the only checker.
  • Get packages and dependencies from a central, validated source, deploy with code, secure the pipelines themselves, and give each builder team ownership of the security of what it builds.

2.3 Reliability

  • Automatically recover from failure, based on KPIs and monitoring.
  • Test recovery procedures.
  • Scale horizontally to increase aggregate workload availability.
  • Stop guessing capacity.
  • Manage change through automation.

Best practice areas: foundations, workload architecture, change management, and failure management.

AWS services: Multi-AZ deployments, Elastic Load Balancing, EC2 Auto Scaling, Amazon Route 53 health checks, Amazon SQS, AWS Backup, and AWS Elastic Disaster Recovery.

Reliability is the ability of a workload to do what it should, correctly and consistently, and to recover from failure. It starts with foundations that fit the workload, an architecture that prevents and contains failures, a workload that adapts to change, and a design that detects failure and heals itself.

2.3.1 Foundations

  • Service quotas and constraints: every account has limits, such as the number of instances or API requests. Know them, monitor them, and raise them before a peak, with room for failover.
  • Network topology: plan IP address ranges that leave room to grow and do not overlap with other networks, use highly available connections to on-premises sites, and connect many VPCs in a way that scales.

2.3.2 Workload Architecture

  • Service architecture: split the workload into smaller services, such as microservices, so a failure stays inside one service.
  • Prevent failures in interactions: define clear APIs, prefer loosely coupled dependencies such as queues, and make operations idempotent, so a repeated request does not repeat its effect.
  • Withstand failures in interactions: retry with backoff, set timeouts, use circuit breakers, shed load when overloaded, and degrade gracefully, for example by showing cached data when a dependency is down.

2.3.3 Change Management

  • Monitor: collect metrics and logs for every component, alert on them, and automate the response.
  • Adapt to demand: scale automatically, and load test to know the limits.
  • Implement change safely: deploy with automation and tested runbooks, use immutable infrastructure, and roll back automatically when a deployment fails.

2.3.4 Failure Management

  • Back up data automatically, encrypt the backups, and test that you can restore them within the RTO and RPO.
  • Isolate faults: run across several Availability Zones, and across Regions where the business needs it, so one failure does not take down everything. Inside the workload, use a bulkhead architecture: split it into isolated cells, like the sealed compartments of a ship, so a failure in one cell affects only the users of that cell.
  • Withstand component failures: monitor every component, fail over to healthy resources automatically, and design for static stability, so the workload keeps working without needing to make changes during a failure.
  • Test reliability: use playbooks to investigate failures, run post-incident analysis, load test, and practice with chaos engineering and game days.
  • Plan disaster recovery: set the RTO and RPO from business needs, then choose a strategy that meets them.

2.3.5 Availability in Numbers

  • Availability is the percentage of time a workload is usable. Each extra "nine" cuts the allowed downtime by ten: 99.9 percent allows about 8.8 hours of downtime a year, 99.99 percent about 53 minutes, and 99.999 percent about 5 minutes.
  • Hard dependencies multiply: if a workload needs two components that are each 99.99 percent available, the workload can be at most about 99.98 percent available.
  • Redundancy adds nines: two independent copies that are each 99.9 percent available, where either one can serve, give about 99.9999 percent.

2.4 Performance Efficiency

  • Democratize advanced technologies: use managed services instead of building them yourself.
  • Go global in minutes, with Regions and edge locations.
  • Use serverless architectures.
  • Experiment more often.
  • Consider mechanical sympathy: use the technology that best matches how you access the data.

Best practice areas: architecture selection, compute and hardware, data management, networking and content delivery, and process and culture.

AWS services: Amazon CloudFront, Amazon ElastiCache, DynamoDB Accelerator (DAX), read replicas, EC2 instance families, AWS Lambda, and AWS Global Accelerator.

Performance efficiency is the ability to use computing resources efficiently to meet the requirements, and to stay efficient as demand changes and technologies evolve. In the cloud, the choice is rarely one product: the best design usually mixes several services, each picked for its part of the job.

2.4.1 Architecture Selection

  • Learn the available services and features, and use guidance from AWS or a partner, such as reference architectures and patterns.
  • Factor cost into the decision, and weigh how each trade-off affects customers and efficiency.
  • Decide with data: benchmark the options and measure, instead of guessing.

2.4.2 Compute and Hardware

  • Choose the type of compute that fits the work: instances (Amazon EC2), containers (Amazon ECS or Amazon EKS, with or without AWS Fargate), or functions (AWS Lambda).
  • Know the configuration options, such as instance families and sizes, processors such as AWS Graviton, and accelerators such as GPUs.
  • Use metrics to right-size, and scale the compute automatically with demand.

2.4.3 Data Management

  • Use a purpose-built data store that matches how the data is accessed: relational, key-value, document, in-memory, graph, time series, or a data warehouse.
  • Tune the store's configuration options, collect its performance metrics, and improve query performance, for example with indexes or partitioning.
  • Use caching for data that is read often, for example Amazon ElastiCache or DynamoDB Accelerator (DAX).

2.4.4 Networking and Content Delivery

  • Understand how the network affects performance, and use features such as Amazon CloudFront for content close to users and AWS Global Accelerator for a fast global entry point.
  • Size private connections to on-premises sites correctly, with AWS Direct Connect or a VPN, and use load balancers to spread traffic and offload encryption.
  • Choose the Regions and edge locations nearest to the users, and tune the network from metrics.

2.4.5 Process and Culture

  • Set KPIs for performance, such as response time, and monitor the parts where performance matters most.
  • Load test the workload, review the metrics regularly, and automate fixes for known performance problems.
  • Keep the workload and its services up to date, and have a process to try new services and features as they appear.

Note: older AWS training groups this pillar into four areas: selection, review, monitoring, and trade-offs. The current framework uses the five areas above. The ideas are the same; only the grouping changed.

2.5 Cost Optimization

  • Implement cloud financial management.
  • Adopt a consumption model: pay only for what you use.
  • Measure overall efficiency: the business output compared with the cost.
  • Stop spending money on undifferentiated heavy lifting.
  • Analyze and attribute expenditure, for example with cost allocation tags.

Best practice areas: Cloud Financial Management, expenditure and usage awareness, resource cost-effectiveness, resource demand and supply management, and optimization.

AWS services: AWS Cost Explorer, AWS Budgets, Savings Plans, Reserved Instances, Spot Instances, S3 storage classes and lifecycle rules, and AWS Compute Optimizer.

Cost optimization is the ability to deliver business value at the lowest price point. It is not only about spending less: it is about spending on what creates value, and knowing where every dollar goes.

2.5.1 Practice Cloud Financial Management

  • Give someone ownership of cost optimization, and build a partnership between finance and technology teams.
  • Set cloud budgets and forecasts, monitor cost proactively, and report and alert on it.
  • Build a cost-aware culture, keep up with new services, and measure the business value that cost optimization brings.

2.5.2 Expenditure and Usage Awareness

  • Govern usage: write policies from the organization's needs, set goals and targets, structure accounts, control who can create resources, and track each project's lifecycle.
  • Monitor cost and usage: tag resources with cost allocation tags, so each cost can be linked to a team, project, or workload, and use tools such as AWS Cost Explorer and AWS Budgets.
  • Decommission resources: find and remove resources nobody uses anymore, and enforce data retention policies so old data does not pile up.

2.5.3 Cost-Effective Resources

  • Select services with cost in mind: managed and serverless services often cost less in total, because they remove the work of running servers.
  • Right-size: pick the resource type, size, and number from real metrics, for example with AWS Compute Optimizer.
  • Choose pricing models and Regions: analyze which pricing model fits each component, apply them across the whole workload and at the level of the whole organization, and consider that prices differ between Regions when the requirements allow a choice.
  • Plan for data transfer: know where data moves between Availability Zones, Regions, and the internet, and design to reduce those charges, for example with caching at the edge.

  • Analyze the workload's demand first. Then shape the demand, for example with a queue that buffers requests or throttling, and supply resources dynamically, for example with Auto Scaling or a schedule that stops development servers at night.

  • Review and analyze each workload regularly, and evaluate new services and features that may cost less.
  • Weigh the cost of the effort itself: a change that saves a little but takes weeks of work may not be worth it, while automating a frequent manual task often is.

2.6 Sustainability

  • Understand your impact.
  • Establish sustainability goals.
  • Maximize utilization.
  • Anticipate and adopt new, more efficient hardware and software offerings.
  • Use managed services.
  • Reduce the downstream impact of your cloud workloads.

Best practice areas: Region selection, alignment to demand, software and architecture, data, hardware and services, and process and culture.

AWS services: the AWS Customer Carbon Footprint Tool, AWS Graviton instances, serverless and managed services, Auto Scaling, and S3 lifecycle rules.

Sustainability is a continuous effort to reduce energy use and increase efficiency across every part of a workload: get the most from the resources you provision, and provision as little as possible.

Deep dive: the six best practice areas of sustainability
  • Understand, quantify, apply: understand the impact of the services you use, quantify it across the whole workload lifecycle, and apply the design principles and best practices to reduce it.
  • Shared responsibility for sustainability: AWS is responsible for building a sustainable cloud infrastructure. Customers are responsible for applying sustainability best practices in their workloads.
  • Measure per unit of work: track the resources and emissions per user or per transaction, so the workload can grow while its impact per unit goes down.
  • Why utilization matters: two hosts at 30 percent use are less efficient than one host at 60 percent, because each host draws baseline power just to stay on.
  • Why it matters to the business: customer demand, government regulations, employee expectations, investors, and competitive position. Like the other pillars, sustainability involves trade-offs.

2.6.1 Region Selection

  • Choose a Region based on both the business requirements, such as latency and data rules, and the sustainability goals.

2.6.2 Alignment to Demand

  • Scale the infrastructure dynamically, so that nothing runs idle, and scale down or stop resources that are not needed, such as development servers at night.
  • Set service levels that match the real need: a slower, cheaper response is fine where users do not need speed.
  • Use buffering or throttling to flatten demand peaks, place the workload close to its users to reduce network traffic, and decommission unused assets.

2.6.3 Software and Architecture

  • Run asynchronous and scheduled jobs when it is efficient, remove components that are rarely used, and make code efficient, so the same work uses less compute.
  • Reduce the load on users' devices, for example by sending only the data they need.

2.6.4 Data

  • Classify data, and move it to more efficient storage as it ages, for example with S3 lifecycle rules.
  • Delete data that is no longer needed, avoid unneeded copies with shared file systems or object storage, and reduce how much data moves across the network.
  • Grow storage automatically as data grows instead of provisioning it in advance, and back up only data that has business value or a compliance need.

2.6.5 Hardware and Services

  • Use the minimum hardware needed, and choose the instance types with the least impact, such as AWS Graviton.
  • Prefer managed services: AWS keeps their hardware highly utilized and shares the impact across all customers.
  • Run GPUs and other accelerators only for the time they are needed, and turn them off automatically afterward.

2.6.6 Process and Culture

  • Adopt methods that bring sustainability improvements into production quickly, keep the workload up to date, and use build and test environments only when needed.
  • Test new features on managed device farms that represent users' hardware, instead of buying many test devices.
  • Measure the impact, for example with the AWS Customer Carbon Footprint Tool, set goals, and track progress.

Problem Statement

3.1 Well-Architected Framework Review (WAFR)

  • A review is a conversation, not an audit. It should be consistent, blame-free, and lightweight: hours, not days. Its result is a list of actions that improve the workload for its users.
  • When to review: early in the design phase, before the go-live date, and after significant changes in production. Better still, the team that builds the workload reviews it continuously and updates its answers as the architecture changes.
  • Who takes part: the people who build and run the workload own its quality, so they answer the questions. Make sure that everyone who knows a part of the workload is in the conversation. To review another team's workload, start with informal conversations, then hold one or two meetings for the unclear or risky areas.
  • One-way and two-way doors: a two-way door is a decision that is easy to reverse, and needs only a light review. A one-way door is hard or impossible to reverse, such as the choice of database, and needs a closer look. This is why the first review happens early in the design.

3.1.1 WAFR Phases

The WAFR process consists of three main phases:

  • Prepare: Set up for success with proper planning and stakeholder alignment.
  • Review: Conduct the actual assessment against AWS best practices.
  • Improve: Turn findings into actionable improvements for your workload.

Reference: Well-Architected Framework Review (WAFR) (AWS Well-Architected User Guide).

The following table shows what the team does in each phase:

PhaseWhat the team does
PrepareChoose the workload and the lenses, invite the right people, and gather the diagrams and design notes.
ReviewAnswer the questions together. Keep a list of the questions that need follow-up research, such as "Did we turn on encryption for the backups?"
ImprovePut the risks in order by business context, plan the improvements, make them, and update the review to show progress. For a risk that you cannot fix now, write a playbook for what to do if it happens, and record the decision in an ADR.

3.2 AWS Well-Architected Tool

  • The AWS Well-Architected Tool is a free tool in the AWS Management Console to review your architectures against the six pillars and adopt architectural best practices.
  • You define a workload, answer the questions of each pillar, and review your answers against the best practices.
  • The tool identifies high risk issues (HRIs) and medium risk issues (MRIs), and builds an improvement plan with videos and documentation for each improvement.
  • You can save a milestone to record the state of a review, and generate a report or see the results in a dashboard.
  • Lenses extend the review for specific technologies or industries. The AWS Well-Architected Framework lens is applied by default, and the lens catalog offers others, such as the Serverless Applications Lens. You can also create custom lenses.
  • The tool supports a continuous improvement mechanism: learn the best practices, measure each workload against the framework, the lenses, and your own custom lenses, and improve by fixing the high risk issues. Apply this cycle to every workload, not only to one.
  • Where the review lives: a workload is stored in your own AWS account, so you control who can see it with least-privilege access. The dashboard shows the workloads of the selected Region, with each one's owner, the number of questions answered, and the number of risks. When AWS updates the framework or a lens, View available upgrades moves a workload to the new version.
  • Sharing: you can share a workload or a custom lens with IAM users, other AWS accounts, or a whole AWS organization, for example with an AWS solutions architect or a partner who helps with the review. Each share shows whether it is pending or accepted, and what it allows the other side to do.
  • Integrations: the tool shows AWS Trusted Advisor findings for the workload's resources during the review, which makes answers faster and more accurate. It also links to AWS Service Catalog AppRegistry, so you can see which applications and resources belong to a workload, and to AWS re:Post for questions to the community.
  • Take a look at the tool in the console: AWS Well-Architected Tool console. You need to sign in to your AWS account first.

3.3 The Well-Architected Tool, Step by Step

3.3.1 How to Navigate to the Well-Architected Console

The AWS Well-Architected Tool is part of the AWS Management Console. To open it:

  1. Sign in to the AWS Management Console as an IAM user with MFA turned on, or with a federated role. Then open the Well-Architected console.
  2. If you are already signed in, choose Services or the search box at the top of the console, type Well Architected, and choose AWS Well-Architected Tool.

3.3.2 Running a Review

  1. Define the workload. Choose Define workload, and fill in the required fields:
    • Name and description: a unique, descriptive name, and the scope and purpose of the workload.
    • Review owner: the person responsible for completing the review, reporting the status of the risks, and tracking the improvements over time.
    • Environment: production or pre-production, to show where the workload is in its lifecycle.
    • Regions: the AWS Regions, and any non-AWS locations, such as on premises or another cloud. You decide what a workload includes, so the Regions are mainly used to search, sort, and filter.
    The optional fields are the account IDs (the tool needs no IAM permissions in those accounts by default), a link to the architecture design so that everyone in the review works from the same picture, and the industry.
  2. Apply the lenses. The AWS Well-Architected Framework lens is selected by default. Add the lenses from the Lens Catalog that fit the workload, up to five at a time. Then choose Start reviewing.
  3. Check the workload overview. The overview shows the questions answered, the risks found, the workload notes, the lenses applied, and the pillar priority. The default order is the order most customers follow, not a ranking of importance. If your workload has other priorities, such as security first for CityCare, edit the pillar priority: the tool reorders the questions and the improvement plan to match.
  4. Answer the questions. For each question, select the best practices that the workload follows. Use Info to read about a best practice. If you choose Question does not apply to this workload or None of these, write the reason in Notes. Choose Save and exit to continue later.
  5. Save a milestone and generate a report. Choose Save milestone and give it a name, for example v1 initial design review. Then choose Generate report to create a PDF of the review.
  6. Read the improvement plan. The Improvement plan lists the high and medium risks, in order, with recommended improvement items and links to guidance.
  7. Improve and measure. Make the improvements, update your answers and notes, and save a new milestone. Compare the milestones to show the number of risks going down.

3.3.3 Reading a Question in the Tool

  • The top of the page shows the pillar and question number, and the question itself.
  • Each question focuses on one key concept that puts a design principle into practice. For example, a cost optimization question about choosing the right type and size of resources (rightsizing) puts the principle "adopt a consumption model" into practice.
  • Below the question, each check box is a best practice. Select the ones that the workload follows.
  • The panel beside the question explains each best practice and links to helpful resources on how to put it in place.

3.3.4 Custom Lenses

  • A custom lens holds your organization's own pillars, questions, answer choices, helpful resources, and improvement plans.
  • You write rules that decide which missing answers create a high or medium risk, and you add your own guidance to fix each risk.
  • To build one, download the JSON template from the tool, fill it in, upload it, and apply it to a workload like any AWS lens. You can share it with other AWS accounts, a solutions architect, or a partner, so every team measures workloads the same way.

No AWS Account?

The tool needs an AWS account. If you do not have one, record the same parts of the review in the review template from your instructor: the workload, the lenses, your answers with notes, two milestones, and the improvement plan.

3.4 AWS Trusted Advisor

  • Trusted Advisor checks resources in your accounts. The Well-Architected Tool reviews the design of a workload through questions. Use both.

Problem Statement

Preview Service

4.1 What the AWS Well-Architected Agent Is

  • AWS Well-Architected Agent (AWS WA Agent) is an AI-powered cloud optimization service that continuously analyzes your AWS environment and delivers personalized, prioritized recommendations across cost, security, performance, and resilience.
  • It generates recommendations at three levels, resource, application, and architecture, with ready-to-deploy automation scripts so that you can act immediately.

4.2 Key Capabilities

  • Automated analysis: unlike point-in-time architecture reviews, AWS WA Agent analyzes your environment on an ongoing schedule. Scheduled recommendations are refreshed every week.
  • Ready-to-deploy remediation: recommendations include prescriptive guidance and ready-to-implement fixes, such as updated infrastructure as code (IaC), not only findings.
  • Goal-aligned prioritization: recommendations are ranked against the business goals that you define. For more information, see Goals and optimization pillars.
  • Cross-pillar impacts: every recommendation shows how acting on it has a positive impact on the other pillars, with an impact category.
  • Trade-off analysis: each recommendation describes the trade-offs of implementing it, including the pillar, the risk level, and a mitigation strategy.
  • Scale: a single agent profile can analyze up to 100 AWS accounts and scan resources in all commercial AWS Regions. For limits, see AWS Well-Architected Agent quotas and limits.
  • Builds on existing AWS optimization services: AWS WA Agent ingests findings from services such as AWS Trusted Advisor, and adds personalization, goal-aligned prioritization, and automation-ready remediation. This reduces the manual triage of optimization findings. For more information, see Related services.

4.3 How It Works

  1. Create an agent profile that defines your accounts, Regions, optimization pillars, and business goals.
  2. AWS WA Agent scans the resources in your configured accounts.
  3. AWS WA Agent generates prioritized recommendations at the resource, application, and architecture levels.
  4. You review the recommendations and deploy the remediations with the provided guidance.
Recommendation typeWhat it targets
ResourceOne specific resource, such as an Amazon EC2 instance, a Lambda function, or an Amazon RDS database.
ApplicationSeveral related resources in a discovered application, with guidance that accounts for how they interact.
ArchitectureIaC templates (AWS CloudFormation, Terraform, or AWS CDK), with recommendations aligned to Well-Architected best practices and deployment-ready updated templates.
  • You can also run architecture reviews of IaC templates on demand, before the resources are deployed, so that you find issues before they reach production. For more information, see Conducting architecture reviews.
  • AWS WA Agent analyzes your infrastructure across four optimization pillars: cost optimization, security, resilience, and performance. Each business goal is linked to one of these pillars. For more information, see AWS Well-Architected Agent concepts and terminology.
  • Note that these four optimization pillars are not the same list as the six pillars of the framework: operational excellence and sustainability are not among them.

4.4 Access and Permissions

  • AWS Well-Architected console: AWS WA Agent is available from the AWS Well-Architected console. For a step-by-step walkthrough of setting up your first agent profile, see Getting started with AWS Well-Architected Agent.
  • APIs: programmatic access is available through the wellarchitected service namespace. For a CLI-based setup, see the API quickstart. For the available operations, see API actions, and for the complete API documentation, see the AWS Well-Architected API Reference.
  • AWS MCP Server: an AI agent configured with the AWS MCP Server can run the AWS WA Agent wellarchitected API operations with your IAM credentials, instead of running AWS CLI commands directly. For details, see (Optional) API quickstart using the AWS MCP Server, and to set up and connect the server, see AWS MCP Server.
  • Read-only access: AWS WA Agent uses an execution role in the profile account and an access role in each account that it analyzes. Neither role lets AWS WA Agent modify your resources: the access roles give read-only access to resource metadata and configuration, and you deploy the fixes yourself.
  • Two kinds of profiles: an AWS WA Agent profile is not the same as an AWS Well-Architected Tool profile, which is a questionnaire that prioritizes the review questions of a workload.

5. The Studio Method

Problem Statement

In the first design meetings, the FlashCart architects jump to their favorite services before they agree on the requirements, and reviews turn into arguments. The team needs a repeatable method that turns a case into a defensible design, the same way an exam question should be solved.

5.1 What an Architecture Design Studio Is

  • A design studio is a working session where a small team takes a realistic case, designs a solution, and presents and defends it, the way an architecture team works.
  • Each studio in this module follows the same method, uses the six pillars to judge the options, and produces the same set of deliverables.
  • The method also works for exam questions: each question is a small case with a business need, constraints, and options.

5.2 The Studio Method

  1. Understand the business context and the requirements: functional requirements, and non-functional requirements such as availability, recovery time objective (RTO), recovery point objective (RPO), latency, compliance, and budget.
  2. Extract the keywords and the constraints, such as "least operational overhead", "most cost-effective", "highly available", or "within 15 minutes".
  3. Map each requirement to a pillar.
  4. Draft at least two options.
  5. Evaluate the trade-offs with a pillar scorecard.
  6. Decide, and record the decision in an architecture decision record (ADR).
  7. Diagram the target architecture.
  8. Review the design against the Well-Architected questions, list the risks, and iterate.

8.4 Lesson Summary
